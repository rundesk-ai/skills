# Developer blogs the owner reads

Read this before shaping a post, and again whenever a draft feels "sectioned off" or less personal than
the owner talks. These are real developers with personal blogs, and they're the models for how the
owner's posts should feel: one person thinking out loud, not a website with sections.

Learn structure, rhythm, and how personality shows up. Never borrow their phrasing, jokes, stories, or
opinions: the content and voice are always the owner's.

## The owner's picks

These are the writers the owner named. When in doubt, ask "would this read like one of their posts?"

### Cliff Harris (Positech, the Democracy games)

Blog: https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/
Example: https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2026/07/28/financial-research-and-ai/

- No subheadings in any post. Paragraph breaks and screenshots do all the structuring.
- Opens mid-thought with a casual interjection or what he's been up to, never a thesis statement.
- Long, rambling paragraphs full of asides, broken up by single blunt lines that land a point.
- Opinions are blunt and framed as his own ("I think", "I know"). A caveat gets one quick line and
  doesn't soften the take.
- Constant self-deprecation and honest admissions about himself. That's where the warmth comes from.
- Ends with a casual call to action and a joke, never a summary.

**What to take from him:** you can be opinionated and personal in plain paragraphs. The admissions and
asides do the work headings would otherwise do.

### Andy Sztark (Citystate)

Devlog: https://www.citystategame.com/devlog
Example: https://www.citystategame.com/post/citystate-ii-postmortem-a-lesson-in-game-design-for-city-building-games

- A long postmortem, so it does use headings: frequent, short, sentence case, often comparisons.
- The sections still read as one argument. Each builds on the last, joined by explicit transitions.
- Confessional voice. He owns past mistakes and states his own taste openly.
- Presents the other side fairly before settling his position.
- Ends by talking to the reader, giving his decision, and teasing what's next. No tidy recap.

**What to take from him:** when a post is long enough to need sections, they're chapters of one
argument, not separate topics. Admitting what you got wrong is what makes it personal.

### Aaron Francis (Laravel)

Blog: https://aaronfrancis.com/
Example: https://aaronfrancis.com/2024/because-i-wanted-to-12c5137c

- No headings in the essay.
- Opens with a short first-person line and builds the whole piece around it.
- Very short paragraphs, rhetorical questions, playful pushback against the obvious objection.
- Talks straight to the reader ("you").
- Ends with a line that echoes the opening.

**What to take from him:** one clear idea, said simply, in short beats, closing back where it started.

## Others reviewed for comparison

- **Dan Abramov,** https://overreacted.io/the-wet-codebase/. No subheadings, short paragraphs, one
  horizontal rule before the close, warm informal sign-off.
- **Simon Willison,** https://simonwillison.net/2026/mar/1/ai-writing. No headings. States his own
  rules in first person, ends on a practical pointer.
- **Robin Moffatt,** https://rmoff.net/ai/. No headings. A tl;dr up top, then bold lead-ins inside
  bullets.
- **Dan Luu,** https://danluu.com/hn-comments/. No headings in his own writing. Long, blunt,
  first-person paragraphs.
- **Julia Evans,** https://jvns.ca/blog/2024/02/16/popular-git-config-options/. About 20 headings,
  because the post is a reference list. Headings are lowercase and casual, and the prose stays personal:
  she admits what she doesn't understand.
- **Josh Comeau,** https://www.joshwcomeau.com/blog/how-i-built-my-blog-v2/. Many headings, but the
  sections read like chapters of one build story told in order.

## What carries over

- **Personality comes from admissions and asides,** not structure. Cliff and Andy both own their habits
  and mistakes right in the text. Keep every one the owner gives you.
- **Endings land the owner's point or circle back to the opening.** None of these writers closes with a
  summary of every section.
