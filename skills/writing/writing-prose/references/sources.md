| Jeanette Sherman, *Why "Slop" Looks So Sloppy* (essay) | practitioner essay drawing on rhetoric and composition | Model-written text is high in cohesion, low in coherence and low-variance in cadence. Coherence is the macro-scale quality of an argument built for its purpose and audience, and it matters most of the three. People are more likely to have coherence without cohesion than the reverse. Abstract nouns as subjects, used paragraph after paragraph, are the most reliable tell; an occasional one is human. Stock devices ("not just x, but y", "Still x. Still y. Just z", "Now here comes the important part:", "honestly?") are what good writers use too, and the problem is their reflexive repetition. Human cadence varies to create emphasis and because thinking digresses. Readers attend to novelty (intentional pattern breakage followed by relief into a coherent point, with a shelf life) and to purpose and meaning. Writing keeps the promises its opening makes (Chekhov's rifle); a callback after a meandering middle rings hollow, and writing through to the conclusion before revising the opening helps. Good writing is directional: it shepherds the reader, blocks the paths where they would wander and anticipates objections. The point, novelty and judgment have to come from a person, because a model's outline from a few ideas comes out low in coherence. | The three C's and the priority of coherence; scrubbing tics does not fix slop (Part I: slop is bad writing even without its tics); judging the strongest reason by the reader (Part II: a model does not know which argument is strongest); Improving a draft, structure before sentences and the hard parts before the pattern scan (Parts II and IV); Before drafting steps 1 and 5; Coherence (shepherding, promises); actors, not abstractions; stock glue and its reflexive use; cadence; novelty and meaning; short-attention forms. The order cadence before cohesion, the procedure, the checks and the debt example come from a working guide written for agents from this essay; this skill restates them in its own words. |
| Wikipedia, [Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) (editors' field guide, linked from the essay) | community guidance | Sentence-level signs: a high density of words such as "delve", "testament", "pivotal"; "serves as" in place of "is"; negative parallelisms ("Not only… but also", "Not X, but Y"); the rule of three; trailing "-ing" phrases that attach vague significance; vague attributions ("Experts argue"); formulaic conclusions about challenges and prospects. | Cut what performs instead of saying: inflation, triads, significance clauses, vague attribution. |
| Digital.gov, [Writing for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing) (U.S. federal plain-language guidance) | public guidance | "Active voice makes it clear who should do what"; in an active sentence the person or agency acting is the subject; a hidden verb (nominalization) is a verb turned into a noun, and "We analyze data" beats "We conduct an analysis of the data". | Actors, not abstractions; the reader as actor in instructions and short forms. |
| Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace* (book) | composition textbook | Readers judge prose clear when the main characters are the subjects and their actions are the verbs; abstractions as subjects and actions buried in nouns make prose read as dense and evasive. | Actors, not abstractions; the subject scan. |
| Digital.gov, [Clear and short](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short) | public guidance | Paragraphs "start with a topic sentence that captures the essence of everything in the paragraph"; "Express only one idea in each sentence"; "Limit each paragraph or section to one topic". | The first words of each paragraph carry the point; one point per line in short forms. |
| Nielsen Norman Group, [F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/) | usability research | Readers scan, fixating on first lines and the "first few words" of lines; put the "important points in the first two paragraphs" and start headings "with the words carrying most information". | Lead with what the reader gets; first words of paragraphs and headings carry the point. |
| George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", *American Scientist* 78 (1990) | journal article | Readers expect a sentence to open with familiar information that links back (the topic position) and to put the weight on what comes at its end (the stress position); they read best when the action is in the verb. | Start from what the reader knows and end on what is new; put the action back in the verb. |
| Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014) (book) | book on writing, drawing on cognitive science | The curse of knowledge: a writer cannot easily imagine not knowing what they know, so they leave out definitions and steps and use private labels; the remedy is to write for a reader who is not you and test drafts on one. | Perspective: the reader was not there; private labels; write for the least-informed reader. |
| H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (1926), "elegant variation" (book) | usage dictionary | Varying the word for one thing to avoid repetition misleads readers into thinking a different thing is meant. | One term per thing. |

| The owner's review of a set of product plans written with this skill, 2026-11-21 | recorded experience | The plans gave software human verbs ("insights learns what every lead is worth", "insights decides where each lead goes", "funnels owns the optimization"). The actors rule passed them because a named service is a concrete actor; nothing checked that the verb fits it. A goal the owner stated for the business ("learn leads, households, policies and binds") had drifted into a capability of the software, which hid who is accountable and described something nobody could build or test. | Give software only the verbs software does; the verb search in the subject scan. The owner's stated aim, that no sentence should leave a reader asking what they just read, and that a person reads each sentence for what it tells them about their own situation, is the basis for the cold read from the reader's seat, which also names the metaphor and noun-stack cases from the same plans ("the brain of the platform", "routing by learned value"). |
