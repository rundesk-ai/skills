# AI tells in fiction

Patterns that make a passage read as machine-written. Check your own suggestions for them before
handing anything back, and never introduce one while editing. When one is already in the writer's
text and it's theirs, a consistent habit rather than a slip, it is their voice: suggest, don't
remove. Several come from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing"
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing); the rest are what model rewrites of
fiction do when they homogenize style (see sources).

- **Stock images.** A breath she didn't know she was holding, something shifted, the weight of it all,
  a silence that stretched, eyes that held a storm, a smile that didn't reach the eyes.
- **Naming the feeling the scene already shows.** A line after the dialogue explaining what it meant
  ("She was afraid, and she knew it"). Cut the explanation you were about to add; never add one.
- **Smoothing the rough edges.** Fragments joined into full sentences, dialect corrected, a run-on
  split for tidiness, every paragraph the same length. The roughness is often the voice.
- **One register for everyone.** Every character speaking in the narrator's polished grammar. Each
  character keeps their own words, contractions and errors.
- **Dialogue tags dressed up.** "said" swapped for "breathed", "growled", "murmured", or an adverb
  added to it. Keep the writer's tags.
- **Inflated significance.** "a testament to", "marked a turning point", "would change everything".
- **Trailing "-ing" commentary.** ", her heart pounding with the realization that…".
- **Reflexive triads.** Three adjectives or three images where the writer gave one or two.
- **Elegant variation.** Swapping a character's name for "the older woman", "the detective" to avoid
  repeating it. Repeat the writer's name or pronoun.
- **Manufactured portent.** Vague, ominous lines that promise meaning without giving any:
  "Something was wrong.", "Nothing would ever be the same.", "And then it began.", "She didn't know
  it yet, but…". One is a choice; the same move again and again is the tell.
- **Dramatic one-liners on repeat.** Paragraph after paragraph ending on a short isolated line, or a
  passage chopped into a run of fragments ("Stopped. Listened. Nothing."). Readers stop feeling the
  emphasis by the second one and start noticing the pattern.
- **Tidy endings.** A closing line that sums up the scene's lesson or resolves what the writer left
  open.
- **Em dashes everywhere.** Keep the writer's own punctuation; don't add dashes they don't use.
- **Chatbot residue.** "Here's a polished version", "I hope this captures your vision".

## In your own report

The same habits show in an editor's reply. Check the report before handing it back.

- **The same phrase in every reply or every bullet.** "Lands harder", "earns its place", "does the
  heavy lifting", "every word is still yours". Say what the edit does, in words chosen for this one.
- **"Not X, it's Y" framing.** "It isn't boring, it's slow at the start." Say the second half.
- **Abstractions as the actor.** "The repetition creates friction", "this choice elevates the scene".
  Name who reads what and what happens to them.
- **Dead-end lines.** Sentences that sound meaningful and tell the writer nothing: "this gives the
  moment room to breathe", "the scene carries more weight", "it creates a sense of unease". Say
  what the reader of the book now sees, hears or learns.
- **A summary that restates the list.** End on the trace check or the open question, not a recap.
- **Uniform bullets.** Every item the same length and shape, each ending on a one-line reason of the
  same pattern. Give the edit that matters more room.
- **Flattery as padding.** "What a lovely opening", "this is strong work". Praise only something
  specific you are keeping on purpose, and say why it stays.
